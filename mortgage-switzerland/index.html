Mortgages in Switzerland for Expats

Buying a home in Switzerland is possible for many expats — but banks set strict requirements on own funds and affordability, and your residence permit decides which properties you may buy. This guide explains the rules before you talk to a bank.

Who is this for? Expats living in Switzerland who are thinking about buying their own home.

Own funds (equity)

Swiss banks only finance part of the purchase price; you need to bring a substantial share as own funds. Under the banks' self-regulation, part of it must be "hard" equity — savings, securities or gifts — and cannot come entirely from your pension fund.

Pension assets from the 2nd pillar and pillar 3a can be withdrawn or pledged for an owner-occupied home. This reduces your retirement savings, so get advice from your pension fund first.

Affordability

Banks check whether you could carry the costs even if interest rates rose. They calculate with a theoretical interest rate that is higher than current market rates, plus maintenance costs and amortisation, and compare the total with your gross income. If the theoretical costs are too high a share of your income, the bank will not lend — even if current rates would look affordable.

What your permit means

  • C permit: you are treated like a Swiss resident and can buy property without authorisation.
  • EU/EFTA citizens living in Switzerland: generally treated like Swiss residents when buying property.
  • B permit (non-EU/EFTA): you can buy a home you live in yourself without authorisation, but not property purely as an investment or a holiday home.
  • L permit and short stays: stricter rules apply, and banks are usually cautious with short-term permits. Check with your canton and bank before you search.

Lex Koller basics

The federal law on the acquisition of real estate by persons abroad (Lex Koller) restricts property purchases by people who are not resident in Switzerland or who are non-EU/EFTA citizens without a C permit. Buying a holiday home as a person abroad requires cantonal authorisation and is limited by quotas. If you are unsure whether the law applies to you, ask the cantonal authority responsible for Lex Koller before signing anything.

Amortisation

A Swiss mortgage is usually split into a first and a second mortgage. The second mortgage must be paid down (amortised) within a set period, at the latest by retirement. You can amortise directly or indirectly via pillar 3a, which can have tax advantages. Ask your bank which option suits you, and compare offers from several lenders. Start with our Finance & Banking guide.

Frequently asked questions

Can foreigners buy property in Switzerland? ▾

Yes, depending on the permit. C permit holders and EU/EFTA citizens living in Switzerland are generally treated like Swiss residents; non-EU B permit holders can buy a home they live in themselves.

How much own funds do I need for a Swiss mortgage? ▾

Banks require a substantial share of the purchase price as own funds, and part of it must be hard equity rather than pension money. Ask several banks for their exact requirements.

Can I use my pension fund to buy a home? ▾

Yes, 2nd pillar and pillar 3a assets can be withdrawn or pledged for an owner-occupied home, but this reduces your retirement savings.

What is Lex Koller? ▾

A federal law that restricts property purchases by persons abroad. It affects people not resident in Switzerland and non-EU/EFTA citizens without a C permit.

Do I have to repay a Swiss mortgage? ▾

The second mortgage must be amortised within a set period, at the latest by retirement — directly or indirectly via pillar 3a.
